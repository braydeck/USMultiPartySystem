# Pyramid Post Builder
 
This skill structures Substack posts, essays, and public-facing articles using the Pyramid Principle — the method that gets writing read, shared, and remembered. It flips the instinct to "build up to" a conclusion and instead leads with it, then earns the reader's belief line by line.
 
## Voice profile: write like Brayden

The generic examples in this skill model a punchy "viral Substack" voice that is **not** the author's. Keep the structure (claim-first, evidence-led), but match the *sound* to these rules, derived from his own essay-style writing (e.g. the "Callais and Culture" piece) and his fiction.

**Cadence — earn the punch, don't open with it.** Build through a longer analytical sentence loaded with specifics, then land a short declarative fragment. His real lines: "It inverts it." / "That brake is now gone." / "Ireland is English-speaking." The hammer lands *after* the accumulation. Use anaphora for momentum: "Which makes reform less likely. Which means the next cycle compounds the advantage."

**Diction — precise, lightly academic, mechanism-named.** He names the gear that's turning: "affective polarization," "one-way ratchet," "structural floor," "the incentive structure." Reach for *engineering, architecture, system, signature, incentives* before any metaphor. Do not invent a controlling metaphor he didn't choose (e.g. "the machine") and run it through the piece.

**Evidence in-line; numbers carry it.** Weave exact figures and named sources into the prose (V-Dem +2.3, Boxell/Gentzkow/Shapiro, a 2024 JHU/YouGov poll), not into footnotes. A statistic is a sentence's backbone, not a decoration.

**Headers are claims or sharp questions, never labels or barked orders.** "Why Winner-Take-All Produces Tribal Hatred." "What If Districts Didn't Matter?" Avoid imperative titles ("End It," "Pick This"); he states, he doesn't bark.

**Stance — confident, sober, a little dry.** Morally serious without preaching. Concessions stated plainly. Second person stays generic ("your political identity becomes defined by opposition"), not campaign exhortation. Imperatives and "you should" are rare; declaratives do the work. Reserve direct calls-to-action for a genuine action (e.g. "go explore the simulation").

**Do NOT (these read as someone else writing):**
- Narrate your own structure. Banned: "Here is the claim this post exists to make," "Here is the climax of the argument," "In this section I'll show." Just make the claim.
- Run an invented metaphor ("the machine," "the engine of X") as a recurring motif.
- Front-load barked imperatives, or use them as section titles.
- Lean on coachy connective tissue ("The good news hiding inside the bad news is…"). Be crisper.
- Address the reader as "you" repeatedly outside a real call-to-action.

**More signatures (confirmed across his essays and academic writing):**
- **Coin and label.** He names a phenomenon and italicizes the coinage on first use, then reuses it as shorthand: *Temporal Aristocracy*, *First-Class Vetters*, *traditionalists / utilitarians / subjectivists*. Give the reader a memorable handle for the idea.
- **Frame early, pay it off at the close.** He sets up a structuring frame in the opening and returns to it in the conclusion (ring composition). The reframe close isn't a trick for him; it's how he naturally thinks.
- **Vary attribution verbs.** Not "says/argues" on repeat. His range: *touted, lamented, decried, effused, surmised, deemed, purportedly*. The verb carries his stance toward the claim.
- **Topic sentence first, every paragraph.** Each paragraph opens with its claim, then supports it. A reader skimming only first sentences should still get the spine of the argument.
- **Steelman before you adjudicate.** He lays out competing positions fairly, even the one he'll reject, then judges. Most of his credibility comes from this evenhandedness; don't skip it.

**Quick test:** read the draft against a paragraph of "Callais and Culture." If a sentence sounds more like a productivity-blog hook than that essay, rewrite it.

## Why most public writing loses readers
 
The default essay structure most people learned in school:
 
1. Context — here's some background
2. Evidence — here's what I found / noticed / researched
3. Analysis — here's what it means
4. Conclusion — here's the point
The problem: readers aren't captive. A Substack subscriber opens your post while waiting for coffee. A Twitter click-through gives you one scroll before they decide. A forwarded link competes with 40 other tabs. If your point arrives in paragraph 12, most readers never meet it.
 
**The fix:** State the claim. Then make the case. Then show the evidence.

## The Pyramid Principle for public writing
 
Developed by Barbara Minto at McKinsey for internal communication, the Pyramid Principle works even better for public audiences because the stakes are higher — no one is *required* to read your post.
 
**Claim first** — the single argument the entire piece exists to make
**Key reasons** — the 2-4 legs the argument stands on
**Evidence and narrative** — data, examples, stories, and comparisons that make each reason undeniable
 
This ensures readers get the thesis immediately, then choose to keep reading because they want to see *how you prove it* — not because they're waiting to find out *what you think*.
 
---
 
## What you need from the user first
 
Before building the post:
 
1. **The thesis** — what is the single claim this piece makes? (If they can't say it in one sentence, help them sharpen it.)
2. **Key reasons** — what are the 2-4 main reasons the thesis is true? These become the sections.
3. **Evidence available** — what data, examples, anecdotes, comparisons, or references support each reason?
4. **Audience** — who reads this? General public? A niche community? Policy people? Practitioners?
5. **Desired reader takeaway** — what should someone think, believe, or do differently after reading this?
6. **Tone and voice** — conversational? Analytical? Provocative? Match the writer's existing voice if they have published work.
7. **Length target** — short post (~800 words), standard essay (~1,500-2,500 words), or long-form deep dive (3,000+)?

## Step 1: Lead with the claim
 
Your opening must state the thesis within the first few sentences. Not "I've been thinking about X lately" — the argument itself.
 
**Formula:**
- [Thing people assume] is wrong because [reason].
- [Phenomenon] is actually about [reframe].
- The real reason [outcome] happens is [claim].
**Weak opening (before):**
"I've been thinking a lot about productivity culture lately. There are so many apps and systems promising to help us get more done. I tried a bunch of them over the past year, and I started noticing a pattern. After a lot of reflection, I think the real issue isn't the tools — it's something deeper about how we think about time."
 
**Strong opening (after):**
"Productivity systems don't fail because they're badly designed. They fail because they optimize for output when the actual bottleneck is decision-making about what matters."
 
The weak version makes the reader wait six sentences for the point. The strong version *is* the point — and now the reader wants to know why.
 
**More examples of strong openings:**
- "The American tax code doesn't need reform. It needs replacement — and the math actually works."
- "Remote work didn't kill company culture. It exposed that most companies never had one."
- "The reason most policy proposals die isn't political opposition. It's that the authors never modeled the transition."
**Common mistakes:**
- **Throat-clearing:** Starting with "In recent years..." or "It's no secret that..." or "I've been thinking about..." — delete all of it and start with the claim.
- **Asking a question instead of making a claim:** "What if our approach to X is wrong?" is weaker than "Our approach to X is wrong, and here's why." Questions can work as hooks, but only after the claim is established.
- **Being vague:** "There are problems with how we think about education" → "Public education optimizes for compliance, not learning — and the evidence has been hiding in plain sight."
- **Hedging:** "It seems like maybe housing policy could be improved" → "Current housing policy makes homeownership mathematically impossible for the median household. Here's the arithmetic."

## Step 2: Build 2-4 key reasons
 
Key reasons answer: "Why should I believe this claim?"
 
Each reason should be:
- **Distinct** — not restating the same point in different words
- **Specific** — includes a mechanism, number, example, or named phenomenon
- **Necessary** — if you removed it, the argument would weaken
**How many:** 2-4. Three remains the sweet spot — it's enough to be comprehensive without losing momentum.
 
**How to order them:**
- **By surprise** — lead with the reason that most challenges the reader's assumptions
- **By logic chain** — each reason builds on the previous one
- **By accessibility** — start with the easiest to grasp, escalate to the most complex
Pick whichever creates the strongest reading momentum for this specific argument.
**Weak reasons (too vague):**
- "The system has problems."
- "People don't like it."
- "It could be better."
**Strong reasons (specific and arguable):**
- "The current system collects $2.1T less than it spends annually, and no combination of rate adjustments closes that gap."
- "73% of users abandon the product within 48 hours — before they ever encounter the feature that retains long-term users."
- "Every peer country that tried this approach between 2005-2020 reversed it within a decade."
**Common mistakes:**
- **Reasons that don't actually support the thesis** — each reason must directly make the claim more believable. If it's interesting but tangential, cut it or save it for another post.
- **Overlapping reasons** — "Users find it confusing" and "The UX is unintuitive" are the same point. Merge or pick one.
- **Too many reasons** — more than four and you're writing a listicle, not an argument. If you have six reasons, some of them are sub-points of others.

## Step 3: Provide compelling evidence
 
For each reason, provide evidence that earns belief. Public audiences need different evidence than internal stakeholders — they need to *feel* the argument as well as see it.
 
**Data (establishes scale and credibility)**
- Statistics, studies, historical records, public datasets
- Cite sources — readers will check, and citations build trust
- Example: "Between 2010 and 2023, median rent increased 41% while median wages rose 17% (BLS, ACS)."
**Examples and stories (make the abstract concrete)**
- Real-world cases, named companies or policies, historical parallels
- Hypotheticals that let the reader see themselves in the argument
- Example: "Consider a teacher in Phoenix making $48,000. Under current policy, she'd need to save for 14 years to afford a down payment on the median home in her district. Under the proposed system, that drops to 6."
**Comparisons and contrasts (create "aha" moments)**
- Before/after, us/them, expected/actual
- These are the most shareable moments in any piece
- Example: "Denmark spends 30% less per capita on healthcare and lives 3 years longer. The difference isn't medicine — it's preventive infrastructure."
**Concessions (build trust by acknowledging complexity)**
- Name the strongest objection to your argument, then address it
- This is the single most effective credibility move in public writing — readers trust writers who don't pretend the world is simple
- Example: "The obvious objection is transition cost. And it's legitimate — modeling shows a 13-year period before the new system breaks even. But the current trajectory hits insolvency in 11."
**Visuals (make the invisible visible)**
- Charts, tables, infographics, and diagrams that carry the argument visually
- A well-placed chart can do in three seconds what three paragraphs struggle to accomplish
- When evidence involves trajectories, comparisons, distributions, or multiple simultaneous variables, a visual isn't optional — it's the strongest form the evidence can take
- See Step 3.5 for full principles on visual argument
**Evidence structure per reason:**
1. State the reason (one sentence)
2. Lead with the most striking piece of evidence
3. Layer in supporting evidence (data + example + comparison + visual where applicable)
4. Acknowledge the strongest counterpoint if one exists
5. Close the section by connecting back to the main thesis
**Common mistakes:**
- **Data without context:** "Revenue was $4.2M" means nothing without a comparison — is that good? Bad? Different from last year?
- **Stories without data:** An anecdote alone is an illustration, not proof. Pair it with a number.
- **Ignoring counterarguments:** Readers will think of objections. If you don't address them, readers assume you couldn't.
- **Over-sourcing:** A Substack post isn't a journal article. 2-4 well-placed citations per section is plenty. Hyperlink inline rather than footnoting.

## Step 3.5: Visuals as argument, not decoration
 
In analytical or policy writing, a chart often *is* the argument. A fiscal trajectory that crosses from red to black, a wealth distribution that shifts over 30 years, a before/after tax burden table — these aren't illustrations of your point. They're the most efficient and persuasive expression of it.
 
The governing principle: **every visual should make exactly one claim, and that claim should be obvious within 5 seconds without reading the surrounding text.**
 
### When a visual earns its place
 
Use a visual when:
- **The claim involves a trajectory or trend** — readers can't hold 30 years of fiscal projections in prose. A line crossing zero is instantly legible.
- **The argument is comparative** — before/after, us/them, current/proposed. Side-by-side visuals make differences visceral in a way numbers in sentences never do.
- **Scale or distribution matters** — "98.8% of households are better off" lands differently as a sentence vs. as a filled bar where the sliver of exception is visible.
- **The reader needs to locate themselves** — bracket tables, income distributions, geographic maps. When readers can find their own position in a chart, engagement spikes.
- **You're asking the reader to hold multiple variables** — if a paragraph requires the reader to juggle three or more numbers simultaneously, a visual resolves the cognitive load.
Do NOT use a visual when:
- A single number in a sentence does the job ("40% of users drop off")
- The visual would just restate what the preceding paragraph already said clearly — that's decoration, not argument
- You're tempted to add a stock image or generic illustration for "visual variety" — analytical readers find this patronizing
### One visual, one claim
 
Each chart, table, or infographic should make exactly one point. If you catch yourself needing a paragraph to explain what a chart shows, the chart is doing too much.
 
**Test:** Can you write a single-sentence title for the visual that IS the claim? If yes, it's focused. If no, split it.
 
- Strong: "Fiscal crossover occurs in Year 16 — debt peaks at Year 13 and the system reaches net creditor status by Year 19"
  → One trajectory chart with three annotated milestones
- Weak: "Overview of fiscal, ownership, and distributional outcomes"
  → That's three charts crammed into one. Split them.
### Visual types matched to argument moves
 
**Trajectory / timeline charts** — for claims about what happens over time
- Fiscal projections, debt curves, wealth accumulation, adoption curves
- Annotate the inflection points — the crossover, the peak, the break-even. These are the moments that carry the argument.
- Example use: "The system is self-funding by Year 9" is a claim. The chart showing the grant line crossing the contribution line is the proof.
**Before/after or comparison charts** — for claims about difference
- Current system vs. proposed, country A vs. country B, quintile comparisons
- Place them side by side or overlaid, never on separate pages/scrolls — the comparison must be instantaneous
- Example use: "Bottom quintile retirement wealth: $384K current vs. $15.6M proposed" — that ratio is almost incomprehensible in prose. A bar chart makes it visceral.
**Distribution charts** — for claims about who is affected and how
- Income distributions, wealth ratios, benefit coverage
- Highlight the relevant segment — shade the 98.8%, call out the quintile boundaries, mark where the median sits
- Example use: "The white-to-Black wealth ratio drops from 6.3:1 to 1.9:1" — a paired bar chart collapsing over time tells the story immediately.
**Tables** — for claims that invite the reader to find themselves
- Tax brackets, take-home pay comparisons, benefit schedules
- Keep tables to 5-7 rows maximum for Substack readability. If you need more rows, bold the rows that matter most or provide an interactive version and a simplified static version.
- Always include a "you are probably here" row highlight or annotation for the median case.
**Scenario / sensitivity charts** — for claims about robustness
- Fan charts, scenario bands, confidence intervals
- These answer the reader's implicit objection: "but what if your assumptions are wrong?" Show the range. If the conclusion holds across scenarios, that's the strongest possible evidence.
- Example use: Four-scenario sensitivity showing crossover ranging Year 10-31 — even the pessimistic case gets there.
**Simplified diagrams / flows** — for claims about how a system works
- Fund flow diagrams, policy mechanism overviews, decision trees
- Ruthlessly simplify. A public audience needs the 5-box version, not the 25-box version. Link to the detailed version for readers who want it.
**Maps** — for claims about geographic variation
- Regional tax burdens, policy differences by state, international comparisons
- Use choropleth maps sparingly and only when geography is the actual variable — don't put data on a map just because it has a location component
- If only 3-5 regions matter, a labeled bar chart is clearer than a map with 50 shaded regions
### Placement: the curiosity-peak rule
 
Place visuals at the moment of maximum reader curiosity — typically **immediately after stating the claim but before the detailed evidence.**
 
The pattern:
1. State the claim in prose ("The system becomes self-funding by Year 9")
2. Show the visual (the chart proving it)
3. Walk through the key details the visual reveals ("Notice that the crossover accelerates after Year 6 because...")
This is the opposite of academic convention, where figures appear after pages of explanation. In public writing, the visual IS the explanation — the prose that follows is annotation.
 
**Never make the reader scroll past the claim to find the visual.** If your section opens with "The fiscal trajectory shows three critical milestones," the chart must appear within the next scroll-length, ideally immediately after that sentence.
 
### Design principles for Substack / newsletter visuals
 
**Mobile-first.** Over half of Substack readers open on phones. This means:
- Minimum font size of 14px on labels and annotations
- No more than 4-5 data series on a single chart
- Prefer vertical or square aspect ratios over wide landscape charts
- Test by viewing at 375px width — if labels overlap or the insight disappears, simplify
**Self-contained.** Every visual must be interpretable without the surrounding text. This matters because:
- Email clients may not render images (include alt text that states the claim)
- Readers screenshot and share individual charts out of context
- Social media previews may show the image without your prose
**To make a visual self-contained:**
- Title it with the claim, not a description ("System reaches net creditor status by Year 19" not "Fiscal Projection Chart")
- Label axes with units
- Annotate the insight directly on the chart (arrow + callout to the crossover point, highlight on the key bar, circle around the outlier)
- Include a one-line source note at the bottom
**Consistent palette.** Use a defined color palette across all visuals in a post (and ideally across your whole publication). Readers subconsciously learn that blue = proposed system, gray = current system. Switching colors between charts forces re-learning and breaks flow.
 
**Annotate the insight, not the data.** A bar chart with every bar labeled is a data table pretending to be a chart. Instead, label only the bars that matter — the comparison points, the outliers, the reader's likely position. Use callouts, arrows, or shading to direct the eye.
 
### Visual density: how many per post
 
- **Short posts (~800-1,200 words):** 1-2 visuals maximum. One anchor chart that carries the central claim, and optionally one supporting comparison.
- **Standard posts (~1,500-2,500 words):** 2-4 visuals. Roughly one per major section, but only where the visual is stronger than prose.
- **Long-form deep dives (3,000+ words):** 4-7 visuals. More than 7 and the post starts feeling like a report — if you need that many, consider serializing.
Space visuals at least 300-500 words apart. Back-to-back charts without prose between them feel like a slideshow, not an essay.
 
### Alt text and email fallbacks
 
Substack sends posts as email. Many email clients block images by default or load them unreliably. For every visual:
- Write alt text that states the claim, not describes the image: alt="Fiscal crossover occurs in Year 16, with the system reaching net creditor status by Year 19" — not alt="line chart showing fiscal data"
- Include a one-sentence prose fallback immediately below the image that captures the key number, so email readers who don't see the image still get the point
### Common visual mistakes in public analytical writing
 
- **The unlabeled chart** — no title, no axis labels, no annotations. Forces the reader to decode it. They won't.
- **The everything chart** — cramming all findings into one visual. If it needs a legend with more than 4 items, split it.
- **The decorative visual** — stock photos, generic icons, or illustrations that don't advance the argument. Analytical readers read this as padding.
- **The uncontextualized number** — a chart showing "$15.6M" without showing what the current baseline is. Comparisons need both sides visible.
- **The desktop-only chart** — looks great at 1200px, illegible at 375px. Always check mobile.
- **Burying the visual** — placing charts in appendices, at the end, or multiple scrolls away from the claim they support. The visual goes next to the claim. Always.
- **The redundant visual** — a chart that says exactly what the sentence above it says. If the prose already landed the point, don't repeat it as a chart. Use the visual to show something prose *can't* — the shape of a curve, the scale of a gap, the range of a distribution.

## Step 4: Section hooks, not academic headers
 
Every section needs a headline that makes readers want to read that section — not just label its contents.
 
**What to avoid:**
- "Background" / "Analysis" / "Evidence" (academic labels)
- "The Problem with X" (generic)
- "Section 2: Fiscal Impact" (report-speak)
**What to write:**
Headlines that contain a claim, a tension, or a surprise.
 
**Weak headers → Strong headers:**
- "Housing Policy Overview" → "The Math That Makes Homeownership Impossible"
- "Analysis of Tax Revenue" → "Where the Money Actually Goes"
- "Healthcare Comparison" → "Denmark Spends Less and Lives Longer"
- "Transition Costs" → "The 13-Year Bet That Pays for Itself"
- "Methodology" → (delete it — in a public post, weave your methods into the narrative or drop them)
**Formula for section hooks:**
- "[Counterintuitive claim]" — "The Cheapest Option Is Also the Most Expensive"
- "[Specific number] + [surprise]" — "14 Years to Afford a Down Payment"
- "[Familiar thing] + [reframe]" — "Productivity Isn't About Output"
---
 
## Step 5: Close with implication, not summary
 
Public writing doesn't end with a "recommendations" table. It ends with *implication* — what this means for the reader, for the world, or for what happens next.
 
**Three ways to close:**
 
**The implication close** — extend the argument one step further
"If this analysis is right, then the question isn't whether the system changes — it's whether we design the transition or let it happen to us."
 
**The reframe close** — return to the opening with new meaning
Open: "Productivity systems don't fail because they're badly designed."
Close: "So the next time you download a new productivity app, ask yourself: do I have an efficiency problem, or a priorities problem?"
 
**The call to action** — tell the reader what to do with this information
"If you're a homeowner in a city with restrictive zoning, attend your next planning meeting. If you're a renter, know your rights under [specific policy]. The math won't change itself."
 
**Common mistakes:**
- **Summarizing the essay** — the reader just read it; don't recap. Add something new.
- **Ending with a question and no direction** — "What do you think?" alone is weak. Pair it with a specific framing: "The question isn't whether X, it's whether Y — I'd love to hear where you land."
- **Trailing off** — "Anyway, those are my thoughts" undermines everything before it. End with conviction.

## Full post structure (Pyramid)
 
### Opening (first 3-5 sentences)
 
- **Hook** — an image, fact, or question that earns attention (optional but effective)
- **Thesis** — the single claim, stated directly
- **Roadmap** — a sentence or two previewing the structure: "Three things make this true..." or "The evidence comes from two directions..."
### Body: one section per reason
 
Each section follows this structure:
1. **Section hook** (claim-driven headline)
2. **Reason stated** (one sentence connecting to thesis)
3. **Visual** (if applicable — placed immediately after the claim, before the walkthrough)
4. **Evidence** — data, example, comparison, layered for momentum
5. **Counterpoint** (if relevant) — acknowledge and address
6. **Bridge** — one sentence connecting to the next section or back to the thesis
### Close
 
- **Implication, reframe, or call to action** — never a summary
- **Final line** — make it land. The last sentence is what readers remember and quote.

## Substack-specific considerations
 
**Subject line / title:** The title is the single biggest factor in whether someone opens the post. It should contain the thesis or the tension — not a label.
- Weak: "Thoughts on Housing Policy"
- Strong: "The Arithmetic of Homeownership Is Broken"
- Strong: "What Every Tax Reform Proposal Gets Wrong"
**Subtitle / preview text:** One sentence that adds specificity to the title. This appears in email previews and on the Substack feed.
- "A line-by-line look at why the numbers don't work — and a proposal that does."
**Length:** Most Substack readers expect 1,200-3,000 words for a standard post. Under 1,000 feels thin for a substantive argument. Over 4,000 needs to be exceptionally well-structured or serialized.
 
**Formatting for scannability:**
- Use bold sparingly to highlight key claims (not full paragraphs)
- Pull quotes or callout blocks for the most important single sentence per section
- Keep paragraphs short — 2-4 sentences for web reading
- Subheadings every 300-500 words to give readers re-entry points
**Visuals in email:** Substack delivers posts as email, and many clients block or delay image loading. Every visual needs alt text that states the claim (not describes the chart) and a prose fallback sentence below it. Never let a visual be the *only* place a key number appears.
 
**Opening hook for email readers:** The first ~2 sentences appear in email preview. If they're throat-clearing, subscribers won't open. Make sentence one do real work.
 
**Internal links:** Link to your own previous posts when relevant — it builds a body of work and increases engagement. But only when genuinely relevant; don't force it.

## Writer reminders
 
- Start with the claim — always, even if it feels uncomfortably direct
- If someone reads only the first three sentences and still understands your argument, you've structured it right
- Section headlines are hooks, not labels — they should make someone want to read that section
- Every claim needs evidence; every anecdote needs data; every data point needs context
- Acknowledge the best counterargument — it's the fastest way to build credibility with skeptical readers
- Paragraphs are short on the internet — 2-4 sentences, rarely more
- The title is the most important sentence in the entire post — spend real time on it
- Never end by summarizing — end by extending, reframing, or calling to action
- Background, methodology, and "how I came to think about this" belong in the middle of the narrative (woven in) or nowhere — never as the opening
- Visuals make claims, not decoration — if a chart doesn't have a one-sentence thesis as its title, it hasn't earned its place
- Place visuals at the curiosity peak: right after the prose claim, before the detailed walkthrough
- Design every visual as if it will be screenshotted and shared without your prose — because it will be
- Limit em-dashes to ~5 per document, reserved for genuine dramatic turns. No spaces around em-dashes (AI tell). Replace most with commas, semicolons, colons, or periods.
- The best public writing doesn't just inform — it changes how the reader frames the problem
