# **01 \- Callais**

# **01 \- Callais: The Map They Drew While the Court Was Still Speaking**

*On the morning of April 29, 2026, as the Supreme Court's decision was still being read into the record, Florida legislators were already voting on their new congressional map.*

That's not a metaphor. The Florida House approved a redrawn map within an hour of the Court's ruling in *Louisiana v. Callais* — a 6-3 decision that effectively ended federal court oversight of how states draw districts to represent Black voters. Governor DeSantis had cited the pending decision as justification for calling the special session weeks earlier. They weren't waiting to see how the ruling read. They already knew what it would say.

That speed tells you something important: this wasn't a reaction to a court decision. It was a coordinated political operation that had been waiting for the legal permission slip.

---

## **What Callais Actually Did**

The surface reading of *Callais* is that the Supreme Court struck down Louisiana's second majority-Black congressional district as an unconstitutional racial gerrymander. The deeper reading is that the Court fundamentally restructured the legal framework that made minority representation enforceable for the past forty years.

Section 2 of the Voting Rights Act operated through what's known as the [*Gingles* framework](https://electionlawblog.org/?p=155758) — a set of legal preconditions that allowed plaintiffs to challenge maps diluting minority voting power. *Callais* reworked that framework in three ways that collectively close the door on future challenges.

First, plaintiffs must now provide illustrative maps that satisfy all of a state's political objectives — including explicitly partisan ones. In the South, where race and partisan affiliation are so tightly correlated that statistical disentanglement is essentially impossible, this means any state can draw a map that eliminates minority representation and defend it as "just partisan." Second, evidence of racially polarized voting must be disentangled from partisan preference — again, a near-impossible standard in states where Black voters vote Democratic at 90%+ rates and the parties have sorted almost completely along racial lines. Third, plaintiffs must demonstrate an objective likelihood of *intentional* discrimination rather than simply showing disparate effect.

Justice Kagan, writing in dissent, said it plainly: today's decision renders Section 2 "all but a dead letter."

As [Jamelle Bouie argued in the Times](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/05/06/opinion/callais-voting-rights-act-discrimination.html), the Roberts Court has spent years weaponizing the constitutional amendments designed to protect Black political participation against that very participation — using the 14th Amendment's colorblindness doctrine as a mechanism to entrench the inequalities those amendments were ratified to dismantle. The Roberts Court didn't arrive at *Callais* by accident. It walked there through *Shelby County* (2013), *Brnovich* (2021), and now this — each decision closing another door. [Election law scholar Rick Hasen](https://electionlawblog.org/?p=155758) has noted that race-based legal responses to *Callais* are now effectively foreclosed, leaving structural electoral reform as the clearest remaining path.

---

## **The Cascade**

Within days of the ruling, six states announced redistricting efforts targeting Democratic-held seats.

Tennessee passed a new map on May 7th, eliminating the majority-Black 9th Congressional District covering Memphis. President Trump personally called Governor Lee to encourage the redraw, writing publicly that it would give Republicans "one extra seat" and that Lee should "push hard." Florida's new map could net Republicans up to four House seats. Alabama filed an emergency motion asking the Supreme Court to strike down its own court-drawn map — the one a federal panel had imposed after the state legislature refused to comply with the previous Supreme Court ruling in *Allen v. Milligan*. Mississippi announced a special session, with Republican officials openly discussing eliminating Rep. Bennie Thompson's majority-Black 2nd District. Louisiana is postponing its primary to allow redistricting. South Carolina's governor signaled he wants the legislature to revisit its map.

[Democracy Docket](https://theconnector.substack.com/p/why-vote-harder-and-other-nostrums) estimates this wave could give Republicans five additional House seats before November alone. Florida's contribution could push that number higher. As Georgetown Law professor [Steve Vladeck observed](https://thinkbigpicture.substack.com/p/roberts-callais-supreme-court-redistricting), the Court moved within days to fast-track certification of *Callais*, waiving the standard 32-day waiting period — an apparent intervention in service of Louisiana's effort to redraw maps before the midterms. Note the pattern: in 2022, the same majority stayed lower court rulings blocking Alabama's unlawful maps, allowing Republicans to use those maps and win every affected district — giving them their exact margin of House control in the 118th Congress. History is rhyming.

The enforcement mechanism behind this wave matters as much as the wave itself. When Indiana Republicans tried to resist — voting down Trump's preferred redistricting map last year — Trump backed primary challengers against seven of them. [Five lost their seats on May 5th](https://www.nbcnews.com/politics/2026-election/indiana-legislators-primary-election-trump-redistricting-state-senate-rcna343321), including Travis Holdman, the third most powerful Republican in the state Senate, and Jim Buck, who had served in the legislature since 1994\. Greg Walker, who had served 20 years and broke down in tears on the Senate floor over his fear for the party's future, came out of planned retirement specifically to make a stand — and lost anyway. $12 million was spent on ads to remove them. The message to every Republican legislator in every other state is unambiguous: resist redistricting and your career ends. The machine is self-enforcing.

The enforcement mechanism runs deeper than the maps themselves. On May 5th, [five of seven Indiana Republican state senators who voted against Trump's redistricting push were defeated in primaries](https://www.nbcnews.com/politics/2026-election/indiana-legislators-primary-election-trump-redistricting-state-senate-rcna343321) by Trump-backed challengers, with $12 million spent on ads across the seven contested races. Among the casualties: Travis Holdman, in the Senate since 2008 and the third most powerful Republican in the chamber; Jim Buck, in the legislature since 1994; and Greg Walker, who had served twenty years and broke down in tears on the floor speaking about his fear for the party's future before losing anyway. The primary system is not just redrawing maps. It is eliminating the legislators who would resist redrawing them.

To understand why this matters beyond the immediate seat count: Republicans currently hold the House by a margin small enough that a favorable Democratic environment should flip it. What's being constructed right now is a structural floor — a minimum seat count that's resistant to normal electoral swings. Even in a wave environment, Democrats would need to overcome not just incumbency advantages but maps engineered to make certain districts unwinnable regardless of turnout or candidate quality.

## **The Normal Fix Is Becoming Unreachable**

The obvious legislative response is federal redistricting reform: a law mandating independent redistricting commissions for congressional elections, prohibiting mid-decade partisan redraws, and restoring meaningful VRA protections. Democrats tried this. The For the People Act passed the House in 2021 and died in the Senate because they couldn't reach 60 votes and couldn't unify around filibuster reform.

Here's the structural trap: the redistricting wave makes the Democratic trifecta — House, Senate, presidency — needed to pass federal reform harder to achieve. Which makes reform less likely. Which means the next redistricting cycle happens under the same permissive framework, compounding the Republican structural advantage, which makes the trifecta harder still.

It's a ratchet. Each turn locks in the previous gain.

[Lee Drutman](https://leedrutman.substack.com/p/the-supreme-court-killed-voting-rights), senior fellow at New America and the most prominent academic advocate for proportional representation in the U.S., put it cleanly after the ruling: "The Roberts Court has spent seven years closing every door. Litigation may buy time. It is not a solution." There's an additional layer that rarely gets named directly: even if Democrats achieved a trifecta, passing federal redistricting reform would require overcoming Democratic members who benefit from their own gerrymanders — Maryland, Illinois — and members who calculate that their own structural advantages are worth protecting. The self-interest problem isn't partisan; it's just more consequential in one direction given which party controls more state legislatures. The For the People Act tried to solve everything at once — redistricting, campaign finance, voter registration — and that maximalism made it easier to kill and harder to defend. A standalone redistricting bill might have been a cleaner fight. It didn't happen.

Representative Jamie Raskin, calling *Callais* a "catastrophe for American democracy," cut to the point: Congress should "authorize multi-member congressional districts with proportional representation systems to prevent partisan shut-outs and drown-outs across the country." That's not the typical Democratic response. It's worth paying attention to.

## **The Good Governance Trap**

Here's the detail that makes this moment particularly bitter: the states moving fastest to exploit *Callais* are precisely the ones that never tried to reform their redistricting process. And the states least able to respond are the ones that did.

Over the past two decades, a coalition of good-governance reformers passed independent redistricting commissions in Michigan, Colorado, Arizona, California, and Virginia — among others. These weren't easy wins. They required ballot initiative campaigns, sustained organizing, and in every case constitutional amendments, because reformers correctly understood that a statutory commission could be repealed by the next legislature. The whole point was durability. Embed it in the constitution, and it survives hostile legislatures.

That durability is now a trap. [Michigan's redistricting commission](https://www.legislature.mi.gov/Laws/MCL?objectName=mcl-Article-IV-6) is constitutionally mandated, and its language is explicit: the commission "shall not be altered or abrogated in any manner whatsoever, by the legislature." Michigan cannot redraw its congressional map in response to Tennessee's. Colorado cannot. Arizona cannot. The states that invested in reform put themselves in a position where they are legally bound by it, even as the states that never invested in reform face no equivalent constraint. Tennessee's legislature drew a new map last week by statute — a simple majority vote. No constitutional process required. No procedural barriers. Just a vote.

Virginia is the sharpest example, and the twist makes it worse: the constitutional amendment Virginia's Supreme Court struck down this week wasn't an attempt to gerrymander. It was an attempt to *respond* to gerrymandering.

Virginia passed a bipartisan redistricting commission by constitutional amendment in 2020, with genuine bipartisan support, as a good-governance reform. Then the national redistricting war escalated. Texas redrew its maps mid-decade. Tennessee followed. As the scope of Republican map manipulation became clear, Virginia Democrats concluded they needed the legislature to regain control of congressional lines — not to lock in permanent Democratic advantages, but to compete in a national environment where the other side was doing exactly that. They passed a constitutional amendment to return redistricting authority to the legislature. Voters approved it 52-48 in an April special election.

The [Virginia Supreme Court struck it down](https://www.vacourts.gov/static/opinions/opnscvwp/1260127.pdf) this morning, 4-3, on a procedural ground with the quality of a trap door. Virginia's constitution requires two consecutive legislative sessions to approve a constitutional amendment, with a general election intervening between the two votes. The legislature's first vote came in October 2025 — but early voting for the November general election had already begun. The majority held that "election," for purposes of the intervening election requirement, encompasses the entire early voting period. Since the legislature voted while early voting was underway, there was no proper intervening election. The amendment was "null and void." Justice Kelsey, writing for the majority, was unsparing: "The Commonwealth submitted a proposed constitutional amendment to Virginia voters in an unprecedented manner that violated the intervening-election requirement. This violation irreparably undermines the integrity of the resulting referendum vote and renders it null and void."

The [three dissenters, led by Chief Justice Cleo Powell](https://www.vacourts.gov/static/opinions/opnscvwp/1260127.pdf), identified exactly what's wrong with that reasoning. Powell wrote that the majority "broadened the meaning of the word 'election' to include the early voting period — in direct conflict with how both Virginia and federal law define an election." Her sharpest line: the majority's definition "creates an infinite voting loop that appears to have no established beginning, only a definitive end: Election Day." The logic of Powell's critique is tight: if "election" begins when early voting opens rather than when polls close, the legislature can never know when it's safe to vote on a constitutional amendment — the early voting window for the *next* election might already be open. The majority didn't interpret a rule. It created one that can never be satisfied.

There's a recursive layer here that makes Virginia the perfect emblem of this entire crisis. Expanded early voting is itself a Democratic good-governance reform — Democrats spent years advocating for longer early voting windows as a voting rights and democratic participation measure. The majority used the existence of that expanded early voting period to define when the election "began," and then used that definition to void a constitutional amendment that 52% of Virginians had approved. A Democratic voting rights reform became the procedural mechanism for invalidating a Democratic vote.

So the full sequence is: Democrats build a redistricting commission in good faith → embed it in the constitution for durability → champion expanded early voting as a democracy reform → Republicans escalate nationally → Democrats try to respond by amending their own constitution → voters approve the response 52-48 → the intervening election requirement (good governance) \+ expanded early voting (good governance) \+ a majority opinion that expanded what "election" means \= the democratic majority's response is void. Three layers of good-governance architecture, stacked into a trap. Their prior reforms are now their constraints, and the escape hatch has been welded shut by the very tools they built to protect democracy.

This is the fundamental problem with piecemeal state-by-state redistricting reform: it only produces fair outcomes if it applies everywhere simultaneously. A national system of independent redistricting commissions levels the field — no state can exploit its partisan legislature if every state operates under the same rules. But a patchwork of state-level reforms simply handicaps the states that adopted them while leaving the others free to act whenever it's advantageous.

Democrats spent a decade passing good-faith redistricting reforms state by state. Republicans spent that same decade doing nothing of the sort. Now one side is constitutionally locked into those reforms and the other is free to act by statute. You cannot solve a national coordination problem with voluntary, asymmetric compliance. The reform only works if everyone is playing by the same rules. They aren't.

---

## **The Asymmetry No One Wants to Name**

This is the structural condition of the current moment: one party is actively using every available lever — mid-decade redistricting, VRA gutting, state court appointments — while the other is navigating those levers as if the rules still apply symmetrically. The moderate position — restore norms, pursue incremental reform, wait for the next trifecta — assumes a symmetry in the political environment that no longer exists.

The good-governance position, that Democrats shouldn't respond in kind or restructure things aggressively when they next hold power, is functionally a prescription for a ratchet that only turns in one direction. That's not a criticism of good governance as a value. It's a description of what happens when one party abandons the norms that made good governance a viable strategy.

None of this is partisan paranoia. It's the straightforward extrapolation of what six state legislatures are doing this week, simultaneously, with the explicit blessing of a Supreme Court that just removed the last significant legal brake.

---

## **There Is a Structural Solution**

I want to be precise about what I mean: not a litigation strategy, and not a legislative fix that requires a trifecta that becomes harder to achieve with each passing redistricting cycle. A design solution.

The core problem with gerrymandering — and the reason the *Callais* cascade is so damaging — is that single-member winner-take-all districts make the drawing of those districts the decisive act of political power. Control the lines, control the outcome. The VRA was one brake on that system. That brake is now gone.

Bouie, the day after *Callais*, [asked the same question from a hotel room at the University of Chicago](https://youtu.be/Ju7BkLCbcVg?si=mvJO3bF4sBxsZU4A): what can we actually do about partisan gerrymandering? It's worth watching before reading what follows, because the answer isn't another lawsuit.

But what if the districts themselves weren't the decisive act?

In multi-member proportional districts — where five representatives are elected from a single larger district — the incentive structure of gerrymandering collapses. You can't pack and crack when multiple seats come out of every district. A party with 40% of the vote in a five-member district wins roughly two seats. Minority voters don't need a specially engineered majority-minority district to elect a representative; they need 17% of the vote in a district where five seats are at stake. As Drutman has written, ["the larger the district, the more proportional the outcome"](https://leedrutman.substack.com/p/the-supreme-court-killed-voting-rights) — and a proportional outcome is one where minority communities don't need protected zones to achieve representation. They just need votes.

This system is called Single Transferable Vote. [Political scientists Michael Latner, Guy-Uriel Charles, and Luis Fuentes-Rohwer](https://www.ms.now/opinion/supreme-court-callais-voting-rights-act-reform-proportional-representation) argued in the days after *Callais* that proportional representation "would guard against racial discrimination while preserving the hard-won gains of the Voting Rights Act" — and that it's the one reform that is inherently race-neutral while still producing representative outcomes. The [American Academy of Arts and Sciences](https://www.amacad.org/news/proportional-representation-federal-state-update) has noted that the *Callais* decision has created "a surge of interest in proportional systems" precisely because PR operates without any reference to race, making it resistant to the doctrinal attacks that killed Section 2\.

And it isn't theoretical or foreign. The United States ran multi-member districts in Illinois from 1870 to 1980 — 110 years. It produced genuine minority party representation in heavily partisan districts and is the closest American democracy has come to proportional representation at scale. The [Fair Representation Act](https://beyer.house.gov/news/documentsingle.aspx?DocumentID=5150), introduced by Rep. Don Beyer of Virginia, would implement exactly this system for congressional elections. It has never been more timely.

Not New Zealand. Springfield.

The answer is to make those districts structurally unnecessary. Make the geographic concentration of minority voters irrelevant to their representation. That's not a legal argument. It's a design argument.

The map they drew while the court was still speaking is a symptom. The disease is the architecture.

But if it's architecture rather than culture, there should be evidence: places with the same language, the same media, some of the same political pressures — where the architecture is different and the results are different. Places where the tribal hatred that now defines American politics simply hasn't developed at the same scale.

There are. One of them is Ireland. That's what the next piece is about.

# **02 \- Culture**

# **02 \- Polarization: The Culture War Isn't Happening To Us. It's Being Produced.**

In 1960, Gallup asked Americans whether they'd be upset if their child married someone from the opposite political party. Five percent said yes.

Today, that number is roughly fifty percent.

That shift — from 5% to 50% in 65 years — is the most important data point in American political science that almost nobody talks about in the right frame. It's not a story about policy disagreement. It's a story about what happened to the social fabric of the country at the level of who we will and won't share a life with.

There's a kicker: that 50% figure now *exceeds* the percentage of Americans who would be upset about a family member marrying across racial lines (11%), or marrying a born-again Christian (9%). [Pew Research](https://www.pewresearch.org/politics/2014/06/12/section-3-political-polarization-and-personal-life/) found that partisan identity has surpassed race and religion as the primary social sorting signal in American life. Cross-partisan marriages — between a Democrat and a Republican — now represent [only 3.6% of all marriages](https://ifstudies.org/blog/marriages-between-democrats-and-republicans-are-extremely-rare), down from roughly 9% in earlier decades.

And it's gotten worse since Pew asked those questions. A [2024 Johns Hopkins/YouGov poll](https://hub.jhu.edu/2024/10/27/snf-agora-poll-september-2024/) found that nearly half of all Americans now consider the opposing party not just wrong but "downright evil." Three-quarters of Republicans who believe Trump won in 2020 think Democrats are evil. About half of Democrats think Republicans are evil. The lead researcher, Johns Hopkins political scientist Lilliana Mason, named it plainly: "Vilifying our political opponents is a signal that we consider them to be enemies instead of opponents. The work of governing a democracy requires compromise and mutual respect."

This is no longer a political problem. It is a social one. And what the data shows is that it isn't inevitable.

---

## **The Pattern Nobody Wants to Name**

The standard explanation for American polarization runs something like this: social media, Fox News, the 1960s culture war, demographic change, economic anxiety, elite failure. These are real forces. But they can't explain the most important fact about affective polarization: **it is not happening at the same rate in all countries, and where it is happening most severely follows a very specific pattern.**

The V-Dem project at the University of Gothenburg tracks political polarization scores across every democracy in the world. Their 2025 data, accessible through [Our World in Data](https://ourworldindata.org/grapher/political-polarization-score?tab=line&country=USA~IRL~CAN~GBR~NZL~AUS), tells a striking story when you compare English-speaking democracies:

| Country | Polarization Score | Electoral System |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| United States | **\+2.3** | First-past-the-post |
| United Kingdom | \-0.28 | First-past-the-post |
| Canada | \-0.4 | First-past-the-post |
| Australia | \-1.16 | Preferential voting |
| New Zealand | \-1.42 | Proportional (MMP, since 1996\) |
| Ireland | **\-2.49** | **Proportional (STV)** |

The United States sits at \+2.3. For context: countries with *higher* polarization scores include Myanmar (3.68), Turkey (3.36), Poland (3.35), Brazil (3.22), and Belarus (3.18). The U.S. is not trending toward its peer democracies. It is trending toward backsliding democracies and authoritarian states.

The US line on the V-Dem chart is essentially flat from 1900 to 2000\. Then it rockets upward. The other countries' lines remain relatively stable. This is not cultural drift. It is a structural break.

## **Then There's Ireland**

Ireland sits at \-2.49.

Ireland is English-speaking. It has a common law tradition. It participates partly in the same media ecosystem. It has no particular cultural advantage over the United States when it comes to political civility — if anything, Irish politics carries its own long history of bitter sectarian and nationalist division that makes America's partisan fights look genteel.

But Ireland uses Single Transferable Vote to elect its parliament. And its polarization score is \-2.49 to America's \+2.3. The gap between them — **4.79 points on a scale where zero is the global average** — is one of the largest between any two established English-speaking democracies.

This is not a story about American culture. It is a story about American architecture.

---

## **The Data Across Countries**

The Ireland case is the sharpest example, but it follows a consistent pattern.

Economists Levi Boxell, Matthew Gentzkow, and Jesse Shapiro [tracked affective polarization](https://www.nber.org/digest/mar20/affective-polarization-wealthy-democratic-world) across nine wealthy democracies over forty years. The United States showed the largest increase of any country — affective polarization up almost **70%** between 1978 and 2016\. Canada, also using first-past-the-post, saw polarization rise more than **50%** between 1988 and 2015\. The UK showed an upward trend.

Germany, which uses a mixed-member proportional system, saw affective polarization **decline by more than 35%** between 1977 and 2016\. Norway and Sweden, also proportional, showed statistically significant declining trends.

[Protect Democracy](https://protectdemocracy.org/work/polarization-extremism-and-proportional-representation/) summarized the research directly: "Affective polarization intensifies in countries with winner-take-all systems and decreases with more proportional ones." The [American Bar Association](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/public_interest/election_law/american-democracy/our-work/proportional-representation/) found that "countries with more proportional systems tend to have less affective polarization, tend to be more effective at managing political conflict, and tend to have lower levels of ethnic-based political violence."

The New Zealand case provides what's close to a natural experiment. When New Zealand switched from first-past-the-post to proportional representation in 1996, researchers analyzed [over 800,000 parliamentary speeches](https://www.fairvote.ca/a-look-at-the-evidence/) spanning three decades. They found a marked decrease in anger and hostility in MPs' language after the switch — most significantly in how the ruling party talked about smaller parties. The political temperature dropped measurably when the architecture changed. Not because New Zealanders became nicer people. Because the system stopped rewarding maximum antagonism.

---

## **Why First-Past-the-Post Produces This**

The mechanism isn't mysterious once you name it.

First-past-the-post forces all political competition into exactly two coalitions. When your only options are two teams, your political identity becomes defined entirely by opposition to the other team. There is no coalition partner to moderate toward, no third-party constituency to persuade, no electoral incentive to be anything other than the sharpest possible contrast to your opponent.

This maps directly onto the psychology of affective polarization. With only two teams, the social pressure to perform hatred of the other team intensifies — not just as political signaling but as tribal membership display. The most extreme partisans set the cultural tone. Everyone else is pressured to follow. The system doesn't just permit polarization. It rewards it.

In proportional systems with multiple parties, this dynamic is interrupted at the structural level. You need coalition partners. Your political identity is not defined purely by who you oppose. The political culture — not by accident but as a direct consequence of the incentive structure — normalizes negotiating across differences rather than maximizing them.

Ireland doesn't have lower polarization because Irish people are more tolerant or more reasonable. It has lower polarization because its electoral architecture doesn't reward maximizing tribal hatred. The system produces the culture, not the other way around.

## **What This Means for the Redistricting Crisis**

It's a political catastrophe.

But it's also a symptom. The redistricting war is possible because Americans have been sorted into tribes that consider each other enemies — which means political actions that advantage your tribe feel justified, and institutional norms that constrain your tribe can be violated. Majority-minority districts, fair redistricting commissions, voting rights protections: these aren't shared civic principles anymore. They're weapons the other team uses.

The culture war and the political dysfunction are the same problem. They have the same source. And they have the same solution.

# **03 \- STV**

# **03 \- STV: How to Make Redistricting Irrelevant**

The map they drew while the court was still speaking was a political act. But it was also the solution to a math problem — one that single-member winner-take-all districts create by design.

In a single-member district, control the lines and you control the outcome. The party that draws the map decides which voters live together and which are separated. This is why redistricting has become the central political battleground it has: not because politicians are unusually corrupt, but because the system gives whoever holds power at the state level an overwhelming structural incentive to use it. The math rewards abuse.

There is a different math. And the United States used it in Illinois for 110 years.

---

## **The Root Cause**

[Post 1](https://[your-substack-url]/p/the-map-they-drew) showed what happened when the Supreme Court removed the last federal brake on partisan redistricting — six states redrawing maps simultaneously, five Indiana Republican senators purged for refusing to comply, up to nine House seats shifting before a vote is cast. [Post 2](https://[your-substack-url]/p/the-culture-war-isnt-happening-to-us) showed what forty years of binary winner-take-all politics has done to civil society: the United States at a V-Dem polarization score of \+2.3, trending toward the company of Myanmar and Belarus, while Ireland — English-speaking, STV-using — sits at \-2.49.

Both posts described symptoms. This post is about the cause and the cure.

The cause is simple: **single-member winner-take-all districts make the drawing of those districts the decisive act of political power.** Every downstream problem — gerrymandering, minority underrepresentation, the elimination of majority-minority districts that Callais just enabled — flows from this single design choice.

Change the design and the downstream problems become structurally impossible. Not harder to produce. Mathematically impossible.

---

## **The Fix: Multi-Member Proportional Districts**

The alternative is multi-member districts with proportional allocation — specifically, a method called Single Transferable Vote, or STV.

In a five-member district under STV, voters rank candidates in order of preference: first choice, second choice, third. Seats are allocated proportionally to those preferences. A coalition with 60% of the vote wins roughly three seats. A coalition with 40% wins roughly two. A minority community that represents 20% of the district wins a seat.

You cannot gerrymander this. There is no line you can draw that eliminates a 20% constituency from representation when that constituency only needs 20% to win. Pack them into a district and they elect more representatives. Crack them across districts and they still elect representatives in each one. The geographic manipulation that makes gerrymandering possible in single-member districts has no purchase in a proportional system.

This is the direct structural answer to *Callais*. The Supreme Court's decision gutted Section 2 of the Voting Rights Act by making it nearly impossible to challenge maps that dilute minority voting power — requiring plaintiffs to disentangle race from partisanship in states where Black voters vote Democratic at 90%+ rates, which is functionally impossible. The legal response is a slow road to a dead end. The design response makes the question moot: in a multi-member proportional district, minority communities don't need a court-ordered protected zone to achieve representation. They need votes. That's all.

---

## **The Math**

The specific threshold that determines whether you win a seat in an STV election is called the Droop quota. In a five-seat district, it works out to roughly:

**votes ÷ (seats \+ 1\) \= \~16.7%**

Seventeen percent of the vote wins you a seat. Not a plurality. Not a majority. Seventeen percent.

This number transforms two different political realities simultaneously.

For **minority voters**: a Black community that represents 20% of a five-member district has more than the Droop quota. They elect a representative without court-ordered line drawing, without an engineered majority-minority district, without VRA litigation. Seventeen percent is enough. A Latina community at 18% elects a representative. A politically organized rural conservative community in a blue state at 17% elects a representative. The arithmetic does what the law used to do — and does it without being vulnerable to Supreme Court decisions about what evidence plaintiffs must produce.

For **incumbents**: your seat no longer depends on the lines drawn around you. If you represent a region, you win. A hostile state legislature cannot draw you out of existence. Your name recognition alone — the thing incumbents accumulate over years of constituent service — typically guarantees you well past 17% in any district that contains your voters. You stop being an employee of the party machinery and become an independent franchise. The Indiana senators who were just purged for refusing to draw a Trump-friendly map would not have been purgeable under STV. Their constituent base was more than enough.

## **What the Pizza Tells You**

Here is the plain-language version of why STV is different from what we have now.

Ten people are ordering dinner. Four want pineapple, two want pepperoni, two want sausage, two want plain cheese.

**Under the current system (winner-take-all):** Everyone votes for their favorite. Pineapple wins with four votes — the largest single group. Six people — the majority — eat a topping they didn't want because the "normal" vote was split among the other options. The minority rules by plurality.

**Under STV (proportional):** We order a large pizza cut into ten slices. If a group has enough people to "pay" for a slice, they get to pick that topping. Pineapple gets four slices, pepperoni two, sausage two, cheese two. Nobody goes hungry. You don't have to convince the whole table — you just need enough people to claim your proportional share.

This is what the House of Representatives could look like. Not one party imposing its preferences on everyone else because it won 51% of one district, but a chamber that reflects the actual range of what Americans want — because the math requires it to.

## **Why STV, Not Party List or MMP**

Proportional representation comes in several forms, and the choice between them matters for more than technical reasons. The two alternatives most commonly discussed — party list systems and Mixed Member Proportional (MMP) — both produce proportional outcomes. Neither is the right fit for the United States, and understanding why clarifies what STV is actually doing.

**Party list** is the most common form of proportional representation globally. Voters vote for a party; seats are allocated proportionally; the party determines who fills those seats, either through a pre-ranked closed list (party leadership decides) or an open list (voters can express preference within the party's candidates). The problem is structural: party list systems *strengthen* party machines rather than weakening them. In a closed list, a party insider who ranks third on the list wins a seat regardless of whether any voter specifically wanted them — the party gave them the seat. In an open list, voters must still vote for a party as their primary act, then express a secondary preference within it.

The political ask in 2026 America — "vote for a party" — is the hardest possible sell. Both parties have historically low favorability. The MAGA movement built itself explicitly against Republican Party institutions. The Democratic base has spent years frustrated with party leadership. Asking Americans to subordinate their candidate preference to a party organizational decision is asking them to trust the exact institutions they have decided not to trust.

**MMP** (used in Germany, New Zealand, Scotland) is more sophisticated: roughly half the seats are elected locally by constituency, and half by party list to achieve proportional overall outcomes. This preserves local representation while adding proportionality. It is genuinely better than FPTP. But it creates two classes of legislators — locally accountable constituency members and party-list members who represent no specific geography — and the list component still puts party leadership in control of who fills those seats. List MPs answer to the party that ranked them. Constituency MPs answer to a place. The democratic accountability is uneven by design.

MMP also requires two separate votes, adds significant ballot complexity, and produces unpredictable legislature sizes when parties win more constituency seats than their list vote entitles them to.

**STV does something categorically different.** Voters rank individual candidates in order of preference. No party list. No party organizational gate on who appears or in what order. The party does not choose your representative — you do. A voter can rank two Democrats in order of their preference, then a moderate Republican third. A voter can rank a local independent first and a party candidate second. The preference expression is personal, not organizational.

This matters for three reasons specific to the American context:

*First, it's already American.* STV doesn't require importing a foreign voting culture. It requires extending a system that American voters in multiple cities are already using right now.

*Second, it preserves competition within parties.* Under party list, if the Democratic Party earns five seats, party leadership decides which five Democrats hold them. Under STV, voters decide — and two Democrats in the same district can compete for preference votes, meaning the more responsive, more locally rooted candidate wins over the more leadership-connected one. This is healthy intra-party accountability that list systems eliminate entirely.

*Third, it creates space for genuine local representation without party gatekeeping.* A beloved local figure — a school superintendent, a longtime community organizer, a business owner known across a region — can run without a national party's endorsement and win by accumulating preference votes from across party lines. A party list requires being on the list. STV requires earning votes. Those are different things.

## **What Australia Learned About Party Lists**

Australia provides the clearest empirical test of what happens when STV drifts toward party-list behavior — and what reversing that drift produces.

From 1984 to 2013, Australia's Senate used Group Voting Tickets: voters could mark a single "1" above the line, and the party distributed preferences on their behalf through pre-registered backroom deals. This is functionally a party-list system grafted onto an STV ballot. The result was a "preference whisperer" ecosystem — professional dealmakers who charged parties up to $55,000 to engineer preference flows that could win a senate seat on as little as 0.5% of the primary vote. The 2013 Senate election produced its most extreme outcome: a candidate winning a Victorian seat with a tiny fraction of the vote through cascading deals while candidates with vastly more actual support were eliminated.

Australia then moved *away* from party-list behavior. The 2016 reform abolished Group Voting Tickets, returning preference control to individual voters. Outcomes became more proportional to actual voter intent. Micro-parties were largely eliminated unless they earned genuine vote share.

Victoria, which still uses the old system, shows the contrast in real time. At the 2018 Victorian election, a Liberal Democrat candidate won a senate seat with 0.8% of the vote, defeating a candidate who had 12.3% — fifteen times more votes. A Transport Matters Party candidate won with 0.6%, defeating the Greens who polled 9%. The party with the better preference deal, not the party with more voters, won the seat. This is what party-list behavior produces.

The argument for Open List PR in America is an argument for deliberately recreating the condition Australia spent a decade trying to escape.

## **On the Drutman Pivot**

Lee Drutman — one of the most important academic advocates for proportional representation, and someone whose work this simulation directly builds on — has recently shifted from preferring STV to preferring Open List Proportional Representation. His argument deserves a direct response because it's the strongest version of the case against STV in the current reform conversation.

Drutman's pivot is primarily philosophical, not empirical. His own writing acknowledges that research on multi-winner STV "was powerful in improving diverse representation." What moved him was a theoretical reframe: he now believes parties as organizational structures are the load-bearing element of healthy multiparty democracy, and that STV — by centering voter preference over party identity — is insufficiently "party-building." He wants parties to be the legible actors, not individual candidates.

The negative empirical findings he's drawing on apply almost entirely to single-winner IRV (instant-runoff voting), not multi-winner STV. The Alaska research on center squeeze applies to single-winner races. The 2024 ballot measure failures — Missouri, Montana, Colorado, Idaho, Nevada — were all single-winner IRV proposals. Using those failures to draw conclusions about multi-winner STV is a category error: they are different systems that happen to both use ranked ballots, the way a bicycle and a Formula One car are both vehicles.

The international evidence points the other way. Ireland has used STV for every general election since 1922 — over a century — survived two referendum attempts to replace it (1959 and 1968), and the V-Dem project ranked it the third most electorally democratic country in the world in 2024\. Its parliament currently seats Fianna Fáil, Fine Gael, Sinn Féin, Labour, Greens, Social Democrats, People Before Profit, and multiple independents in meaningful numbers. That is not a broken two-party doom loop.

More fundamentally, Open List PR does not generalize to the American institutional architecture. The Senate is structurally single-winner per seat per election cycle — OLPR has nothing to say about Senate races, and you're back to needing ranked-choice single-winner systems for them regardless. The presidency is similarly resistant. STV with Condorcet or IRV scales coherently across all three institutions. OLPR addresses the House and then stops.

There is also a mechanical advantage STV holds for new party formation that the OLPR camp understates. In a five-seat district under OLPR, a voter whose party fails to clear the \~17% Droop threshold loses their vote entirely. Under STV, that vote transfers to their next preference — meaning voting for a minor party that doesn't reach quota isn't wasted, it flows onward. STV is actually more forgiving of early-stage new party entry than OLPR in the same district sizes.

The STV ballot measures haven't failed. They haven't been tried. Every ranked-choice initiative that has appeared on a state ballot has been single-winner IRV — a different product, a more modest reform, and one that doesn't change the fundamental structure of representation. Proportional multi-winner STV has never been put to a major statewide American vote in the modern era.

And in any case, ballot measures aren't the right vehicle. This needs to be federal to matter.

## **It's Already Here**

STV is not a European import or a theoretical construct. American voters are using it right now.

**Portland, Oregon** implemented multi-winner STV for its city council in 2024 — twelve councilors elected from four districts of three seats each. Portland's implementation is the most significant recent American example: a major city, a competitive political environment, a proportional system producing representation that genuinely reflects the city's political range.

**Cambridge, Massachusetts** has used STV to elect its city council and school board since **1941** — eighty-five years of uninterrupted proportional representation in an American city. Cambridge is not a pilot program. It is institutional memory.

**Minneapolis, Minnesota** and **Albany, California** use ranked-choice voting systems, joining a growing list of American municipalities where voters rank candidates rather than choosing one.

Internationally, Ireland and Australia use proportional RCV for national elections — Ireland's Dáil, Australia's Senate — and Scotland uses STV for local government. These are functioning democracies with long institutional histories, not experiments.

The Illinois multi-member district system that ran from 1870 to 1980 adds a further 110 years of American precedent at the state legislative level. The domestic evidence base is not thin. It spans continents, decades, and every level of government from city council to national parliament.

## **The Illinois Precedent**

The United States ran multi-member districts in Illinois for 110 years — from 1870 to 1980\. Illinois used a system called cumulative voting, a close cousin of STV, in which voters could concentrate their votes on a single candidate or spread them across multiple candidates according to their preferences.

The results were consistent across eleven decades. Republican voters in heavily Democratic Chicago elected Republican representatives. Democratic voters in heavily Republican downstate Illinois elected Democratic representatives. The minority party in any given region got seats — not because courts required it, but because the math guaranteed it. Political scientists who study the Illinois system find that it produced more competitive, more genuinely representative outcomes than comparable single-member systems in other states.

The Illinois experiment didn't collapse into chaos. It didn't produce a fractured, ungovernable legislature. It ran for 110 years until a referendum ended it — pushed primarily by incumbent legislators who found proportional representation too competitive for their comfort. The system wasn't ended because it failed. It was ended because it worked, and working meant nobody could protect their seat by controlling a map.

This is not New Zealand. This is not Germany or Ireland. This is Springfield.

## **The Fair Representation Act: This Is Already a Bill**

Everything described in this post is not just a theoretical proposal. It is live legislation.

The [Fair Representation Act](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/house-bill/4632) — reintroduced in July 2025 by Reps. Don Beyer and Jamie Raskin — would implement multi-winner STV for all U.S. House elections. Congress has clear authority here under Article I Section 4, the Elections Clause, which explicitly grants Congress power to override state rules for federal elections — no constitutional amendment required. The mechanism is exactly what this post describes: five-member districts as the standard, ranked-choice ballots, Droop quota allocation, independent redistricting commissions drawing the district lines.

The Fair Representation Act has stalled not because the policy is wrong but because the advocacy coalition hasn't been able to show people what it would actually produce. What does a proportional House look like? What legislation passes? What does the governing math look like when nine parties need to form working coalitions? Those questions have been unanswerable from inside the existing two-party framework.

The simulation in [Post 4](https://[your-substack-url]/p/what-60000-americans-revealed) is the empirical case the Fair Representation Act has been missing. Nine parties. Specific vote counts. Specific bill outcomes. A governing president with a specific policy profile. The abstract argument for proportional representation becomes a concrete picture of what it produces.

The bill exists. The sponsor exists. The precedents exist. What the proposal has needed is evidence — and what makes the current moment uniquely urgent is that *Callais* has created a redistricting crisis that makes the structural argument for the Fair Representation Act easier to make than it has ever been before.

## **Doubling the House**

The Fair Representation Act also combines STV with an expansion of the House to approximately 870 members — one representative per roughly 380,000 constituents, closer to the Founders' intended ratio.

The House has been frozen at 435 members since 1929, when the country had 123 million people. It now has 335 million. The average district has grown from 280,000 to 760,000 constituents. The expansion to 870 members isn't just a representational fix — it compounds the anti-gerrymandering effect.

In a 435-member House with single-member districts, a state with 10 seats has 10 lines to draw and manipulate. In an 870-member House with five-member proportional districts, the same state has two districts — and both of those districts are proportional. The number of manipulable line-drawing decisions drops from 10 to 2\. The manipulations that remain are mathematically constrained. You've changed the game, not just the rules.

The doubling also has a specific political economy that makes it more sellable than it sounds: existing members run in larger districts where they are the most recognizable names, and their threshold is 17%. The reform that would eliminate gerrymandering also makes the incumbents who would have to vote for it safer in their seats. That's not an accident. It's how you get turkeys to vote for Thanksgiving.

# **04 \- Pres Primary**

# **04 \- Presidential Primary or how to select the most representative president possible**

Iowa has 3.2 million people. It is 88% white — roughly 30 percentage points whiter than the national average. It produces corn, soybeans, and, for the past fifty years, the de facto first verdict on who gets to run for president of the United States.

This is not an accident of geography or tradition that outlasted its usefulness. It is a structural discrimination baked into the presidential selection system — one with a name, a constitutional analysis, and a fix. The name, borrowed from the white paper underlying this series, is the Temporal Aristocracy: a class of First-Class Vetters, whose participation determines whether candidates remain viable, and Second-Class Ratifiers, whose participation ratifies whatever the vetters permitted to survive.

A California voter who wanted to support a candidate Iowa eliminated never got a meaningful vote. A New Jersey voter whose preferred candidate ran out of money after New Hampshire has been effectively disenfranchised in the nomination contest. There is no rational basis for this hierarchy. It is geography functioning as a qualification for political influence — which is precisely what the 14th Amendment was written to prohibit.

## **Three Discriminations, One System**

The Presidential Election Equality Act framework — the constitutional architecture for presidential primary reform — identifies three distinct structural discriminations operating simultaneously in the current system.

**Temporal discrimination:** Iowa and New Hampshire possess a combined electorate of roughly 4.5 million voters — less than 2% of the national electorate — with permanent first-mover veto power over the entire presidential field. Candidates who fail to survive these states are typically eliminated before most Americans have paid any attention. The sequence produces what the Supreme Court established in [*United States v. Classic* (1941)](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/313/299/) is constitutionally impermissible: a tiered citizenship where early-state voters have qualitatively different influence over a federal election than late-state voters.

**Financial discrimination:** The current system allows candidates to ignore the entire electorate and concentrate resources on "Donor Hubs" — Manhattan, the Bay Area, K Street, Silicon Valley. Campaign finance is financial gerrymandering. A candidate who raises $40 million from 3,000 donors in two zip codes is not demonstrating national viability. They are demonstrating access to concentrated wealth. This is the mechanism by which a Nationalist donor class representing 2.5% of the electorate can put a candidate on the field as if they represented a mass constituency.

**Mechanical discrimination:** Winner-take-all plurality counting in the general election discards the expressed preferences of millions of voters. A Republican in California, a Democrat in Texas — their vote for president is mathematically inert. The ballot design transmutes their preference into a zero.

All three discriminations operate through the same underlying logic: the system selects for a particular kind of winner, and that kind of winner is not the candidate with the broadest genuine support.

## **What the Current System Actually Selects For**

[Post 4](https://[your-substack-url]/p/what-60000-americans-revealed) showed that the primary system rewards intensity-faction dominance over broad-coalition appeal — the mechanism by which a small, well-funded ideological faction can control nominations despite representing a fraction of the electorate.

The presidential primary is the clearest example of this dynamic operating at the highest level.

The current system rewards:

**Intensity over breadth.** A candidate who generates passionate commitment from 30% of primary voters beats one who generates moderate enthusiasm from 65%. In a crowded field, the plurality winner is often the one who has activated the most motivated minority — not the one most voters would find broadly acceptable.

**Small-dollar fundraising capacity.** Which rewards candidates who can activate the ideologically committed and emotionally engaged, not candidates who can build governing coalitions. The candidate who raises $20 million in small donations from the most activated partisan slice is advantaged over the candidate who builds broad but less intense support.

**Media performance.** The nationalized primary environment rewards candidates who generate attention and emotional response, systematically advantaging performers over legislators and builders.

**Iowa.** A state that is 88% white and whose electorate over-represents evangelical Christians and agricultural interests selects for candidates optimized for that specific audience — not the national median.

The people who thrive in this environment are systematically different from the people who would produce the broadest governing coalitions. The smoke-filled rooms that preceded the modern primary system were, whatever their vices, running an informal Condorcet tournament: selecting candidates broadly acceptable to governing coalitions rather than optimized for intensity-faction activation. FDR in 1932\. Eisenhower in 1952\. LBJ with enough political cover to push civil rights legislation through a Congress that would have stopped a more ideologically pure candidate at the door.

Run the 1964 Civil Rights Act through a contemporary primary environment: LBJ pushing civil rights triggers a massive white working-class revolt in Southern Democratic primaries. Dirksen's cooperation on cloture becomes a Republican primary liability. The segregationist faction gets full expression rather than being managed by party structure. The Civil Rights Act was possible partly because the people who needed to deliver it were insulated from their most intense opposition. The modern primary gives that opposition a veto.

## **Democrats Already Tried to Fix This**

For 2024, the Democratic National Committee did something meaningful: it stripped Iowa of its first-in-the-nation status and moved South Carolina to the front of the primary calendar. The intent was sound. South Carolina has a large Black electorate and is far more demographically representative of the actual Democratic coalition than Iowa or New Hampshire. It was a genuine recognition that a 92% white state should not have permanent veto power over a party whose primary electorate is roughly 40% voters of color.

The reform deserves credit as an honest attempt to address the Temporal Aristocracy. And it is still insufficient — not because the intent was wrong but because the structural flaw runs deeper than which state goes first.

South Carolina is one state. It has one demographic profile, one set of regional concerns, one media market, one political culture. Moving the veto from a 92% white electorate to a different single-state electorate changes the identity of the First-Class Vetters without eliminating the category. A candidate who loses South Carolina is still effectively eliminated before the vast majority of Americans have voted. A candidate who wins South Carolina still generates the narrative momentum, donor cascade, and media inevitability that makes subsequent contests difficult to contest on the merits.

The 2024 reform also faced a specific problem: Biden ran essentially unopposed, so the new calendar was never stress-tested against a real competitive field. South Carolina as a genuine vetting mechanism — rather than a ratification event for an incumbent — remains untested.

What the Democratic reform correctly identified is that demographic representation in the early states matters. What it didn't address is that the single-state model itself is the problem. The pod system doesn't replace Iowa with South Carolina. It replaces the single-state snapshot with a **national cross-section** — six demographically diverse small states drawn by lottery in Round 1, then four sequentially balanced geographic pods where every anchor's ideological center of gravity is deliberately counterweighted. The result is a primary process that takes a rolling national temperature rather than a series of regional verdicts that compound through narrative momentum.

The difference between the Democratic reform and the pod system is the difference between choosing a more representative single photograph and building a film. One snapshot, however carefully chosen, cannot capture a country of 330 million people across 50 states. A rolling sequence that cycles through every region and demographic configuration — under rules designed to prevent any single geography from setting a lasting narrative — can.

## **The Rolling Pod Solution**

The American Mosaic Presidential Primary replaces the geographic aristocracy with a system designed around one rule: **every qualifying round must be culturally and demographically balanced.** No single region — not the South, not the coasts, not the evangelical heartland — may dominate any phase of the selection process.

The structure operates in four rounds, May through August, before the November general election.

**Round 1: The Retail Six (May)**

Six small states are selected by lottery — one from each of six categorical diversity buckets — to conduct the opening qualification round. The buckets are deliberately constructed to ensure demographic and ideological range:

| Bucket | Purpose | Eligible States |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| Southwest | Hispanic population, water policy, labor | NM, NV |
| Appalachia/South | Rural poverty, racial history, white working class | WV, AR, MS |
| East Coast | Institutional establishment, corporate governance | DC, DE |
| Midwest | Agriculture, trade, evangelical voters | NE, KS, IA |
| Yankee | Independent voters, high education, secular skepticism | VT, RI, ME, NH |
| Frontier | Indigenous rights, energy extraction, libertarianism | AK, HI, PR, WY, MT, ND, SD, ID |

No large state participates in Round 1\. Iowa may participate — but as one lottery draw among several Midwest states, not as the permanent anchor of the national process. The lottery resets every cycle. Iowa will not permanently hold first position.

The Retail Six produces a Top 12 who advance to the pod rounds.

**Rounds 2–4: The Geographic Pods (June–August)**

Four pods, each anchored by one of the four largest states (Texas, Florida, New York, California) and paired with counterweights specifically chosen to neutralize that anchor's ideological center of gravity:

- **Pod A: "The Cowboy and the Cosmopolitans"** — Texas anchored by Illinois, Washington, Virginia. Texas conservatism versus urban-tech liberalism. A candidate who survives Pod A has demonstrated the capacity to appeal across the sharpest ideological fault line in American politics.  
- **Pod B: "The Sunshine and the Rust"** — Florida anchored by Pennsylvania, Massachusetts, Tennessee. Sun Belt populism versus old-economy industrialism.  
- **Pod C: "The Empire and the Heartland"** — New York anchored by Georgia, Ohio, Arizona. Coastal finance and media versus the rural South and industrial Midwest.  
- **Pod D: "The Golden State and the Guardrails"** — California anchored by North Carolina, Michigan, New Jersey. Progressive California deliberately paired with pragmatic and conservative institutional states.

The pods rotate on a 12-year cycle. No anchor state permanently holds an early position. California will not always vote last. Texas will not always vote first.

**The Scoring and Winnowing**

Each round uses STV — the same proportional ranking system described in [Post 3](https://[your-substack-url]/p/how-to-make-the-redistricting-wars) — with an accumulative approval variant that captures full preference signals rather than plurality winners. The most broadly acceptable candidate advances, not merely the most intense.

- After Round 1: Top 12 advance  
- After Round 2: Top 10 advance  
- After Round 3: Top 8 advance  
- After Round 4: Top 5 advance to the general election

The generous early thresholds protect late-developing candidates. A candidate who is strong in California and Florida but unknown in a Midwest Retail Six state should not be eliminated before the electorate has evaluated them. The current system kills those candidates in February. The pod system gives them until August.

**The Geographic Firewall (Democracy Dollars)**

A candidate cannot use donations pledged by California voters to win in Iowa. Funds from each pod are frozen until the campaign reaches that pod — then unlock 60% for the race and hold 40% in reserve for the general. If a candidate drops out before a pod unlocks, donors get a full refund. This prevents the financial gerrymandering of donor-hub-funded early-state carpet-bombing.

## **The General: Finding the Consensus Winner**

The five finalists face a Condorcet general election — a round-robin ranking that identifies the candidate who beats every other candidate in head-to-head matchups.

This is different from both the current plurality system and from Instant Runoff Voting. Plurality produces a winner with the most first-choice votes even if most voters prefer someone else. IRV eliminates candidates sequentially and can produce counterintuitive results if the order of elimination matters. Condorcet finds the candidate who, when placed in a direct matchup against every alternative, is preferred by a majority — the genuine consensus choice.

The simulation described in [Post 4](https://[your-substack-url]/p/what-60000-americans-revealed) runs both IRV and Condorcet for the presidential general. Both produce the same winner: the Social Democrat / Conservative coalition candidate — a specific governing profile with abortion policy at 14.2 weeks, border enforcement plus Dreamers, EPA climate regulation plus continued production, Medicaid expansion, moderate tax increases. Not a centrist blob. A coherent governing program that commands majority support in pairwise comparisons against every alternative on offer.

That candidate does not emerge from the current system. The current system selected for the candidate Iowa liked in January.

[Post 4](https://[your-substack-url]/p/what-60000-americans-revealed) showed that the Nationalist faction holds 22 House seats under fair proportional representation — a well-funded minority unable to govern alone. The presidential primary is the mechanism through which that faction punches far above its electoral weight.

In a low-turnout Iowa caucus dominated by the most activated Republican base, a Nationalist-aligned candidate with intense minority support can win with 25-30% of the vote in a crowded field. That win generates media narrative, donor momentum, and the perception of viability. The structural advantages of the Temporal Aristocracy — the cascade of expectations, endorsements, and money that flows from an early-state win — then carry that candidate past voters who would reject them in a more representative contest.

Under the pod system, Pod A — Texas anchored by Illinois, Washington, and Virginia — is the Nationalist graveyard. Their candidate may win Texas. They will not survive the cosmopolitan counterweights that Texas is deliberately paired with. The cross-regional appeal that the pod system requires is precisely what a faction representing 2.5% of the electorate cannot demonstrate.

The pod system doesn't produce a different kind of politics by accident. It produces it by design — the same way the current system produces Nationalist-compatible candidates by design, through the accident of Iowa's demographics and the financial incentives of donor-hub fundraising.

## **The Nine Parties**

## **The Presidency: Three Scenarios, Three Presidents**

The presidential simulation is where the two counting methods diverge — and where that divergence illuminates what proportional voting is actually doing.

Under Instant Runoff Voting, the winner of the presidential general is **CON/SD** — a Conservative-dominant candidate who has built a coalition that also speaks to Social Democrat voters. Under Condorcet (Ranked-Pairs), the winner is **SD/CON** — a Social Democrat-dominant candidate whose coalition speaks to Conservative voters. They are separated by 223 votes out of 45,000. A margin of 0.5%. In factor space, they sit within 0.1 of each other on every dimension — security orientation, religious traditionalism, electoral trust, government trust, and populist conservatism.

The divergence reflects how the two methods work differently. IRV rewards first-choice accumulation: the Conservative label draws more first-preference votes from the CON base and survives elimination rounds until facing CON/STY in the final. Condorcet rewards head-to-head performance across the full field: the SD-leading identity wins marginally more pairwise matchups because it sits slightly closer to the nine-party centroid. The result is two candidates from different parties who would govern almost identically — signing border enforcement, DACA pathways, the 2017 tax extension, universal background checks, and a $150B infrastructure bill; vetoing ACA repeal under either identity because the SD coalition partner cannot survive the dismantling of coverage for tens of millions.

They diverge only at the margins. Medicaid work requirements (58.4% mixed-electorate support) and student loan forgiveness (49.7%) are the two bills most likely to flip depending on whether a Conservative or Social Democrat identity is leading the ticket. These are the bills where the label matters. On everything else, the math produces the same result regardless of which cross-aisle candidate sits in the Oval Office.

**The pure-party scenario produces a different answer entirely.** When candidates are restricted to single-party nominees — no cross-coalition blends — both IRV and Condorcet converge on **Solidarity (STY)** as president. STY wins as the spatial median of the nine-party field. Under IRV, LIB is eliminated first, then CON, then SD, leaving STY to prevail over CTR 50.54% to 49.46% in the final round. Under Condorcet, STY wins the same head-to-head matchups. A working-class, majority-female, majority-non-white, 37.7% born-again evangelical candidate — the party nobody currently represents — is the genuine consensus choice of a nine-party American electorate when forced to rank among pure-party nominees.

The STY president signs student loan forgiveness, legal status for undocumented immigrants, and Medicaid expansion without work requirements. They veto asylum denial at the border, police funding increases, work requirements, and the TikTok ban.

Three scenarios, three distinct presidents, two of whom would govern within a few bills of each other. What the simulation shows is that the cross-aisle centroid — the governing position with the broadest majority support — is always within reach under proportional competition. The question is only which party gets to claim it.

# **06 \- CRO**

# **06 \- Congressional Regulation Office: Give Congress its brain back**

In 2024, the Supreme Court decided *Loper Bright Enterprises v. Raimondo* and overturned *Chevron v. NRDC* — a forty-year-old precedent that had allowed federal agencies to interpret ambiguous statutes when writing regulations. Under *Chevron*, if Congress passed a vague law and the EPA filled in the technical details, courts would defer to the agency's expertise. Under *Loper Bright*, they will not. Courts must now interpret statutory language themselves, without deferring to agency expertise.

This sounds like a procedural adjustment. It is the beginning of an operating system crash.

For ninety years, Congress has governed through a workaround: pass vague authorizing language and delegate the actual rule-writing to executive agencies. "Make air clean." "Ensure safe drugs." "Protect navigable waters." The agencies — staffed by scientists, economists, and engineers — translated those mandates into specific, enforceable technical standards. *Chevron* was the legal guarantee that this system would hold. *Loper Bright* removed it. And the Non-Delegation cases now working their way toward the Supreme Court could go further still, potentially voiding entire categories of regulatory authority that Congress handed to agencies over the past century.

When that happens — and the Court's current trajectory suggests it is when, not if — Congress will face a binary choice: let the country's regulatory infrastructure collapse, or develop the institutional capacity to write technical law itself. It currently cannot do the latter. It has the staffing levels of a 1970s legislature governing a 2020s economy, making policy on artificial intelligence with staff who struggle to define it, regulating bioengineering with committees that haven't held a substantive hearing on CRISPR in years.

This post is about the architecture that makes the rest of this series governable. Electoral reform elects a better legislature. This is what gives that legislature the tools to do anything with the power it holds — and what restores each branch of government to its proper constitutional function.

The current system has produced a profound confusion of roles. Executive agencies write rules (a legislative function), enforce those rules (an executive function), and adjudicate violations of those rules through in-house Administrative Law Judges (a judicial function) — all within the same institution. The result is an executive branch that is simultaneously legislator, prosecutor, and judge in its own cases, while Congress has become a performative body and courts have been told to defer to agency expertise rather than interpret the law.

The Governmental Efficacy Act untangles this. Congress writes the law. The executive enforces it. The judiciary adjudicates disputes. Each branch gets its constitutional job back.

## **Why DOGE Failed**

Before the solution, the cautionary tale.

DOGE's operating theory was simple: government is bloated, so fire the bloat, and the remaining people will work harder and produce better outcomes. In practice, firing inspectors doesn't eliminate the legal requirement for inspections — it just produces a backlog. Eliminating permit processors doesn't remove the statutory permit requirements — it produces longer lines. Cutting agency staff doesn't change the laws those agencies are executing; it just degrades the execution.

**You cannot fire your way to efficiency if the bottleneck is the law.**

The regulatory system is slow not primarily because federal employees are lazy but because the laws they're executing are vague, contradictory, and decades out of date. Drug approval timelines are long partly because FDA staff are insufficiently motivated and partly because the statutory framework governing drug approval was written in 1962\. AI companies operate in a regulatory vacuum not because the FTC lacks personnel but because the laws governing consumer protection were written before the internet existed. Firing the person stamping the form doesn't remove the legal requirement for the stamp.

The fix isn't personnel management. It's rebuilding the factory.

## **The Congressional Regulation Office**

The Governmental Efficacy Act establishes the **Congressional Regulation Office** — a non-partisan legislative civil service of approximately 45,000 technical experts, transferred from executive agencies to the legislative branch.

The transfer is specific and surgical. What moves to the CRO is the policy-formulation capacity of the regulatory state — the scientists, economists, and engineers who write the technical specifications for regulations. What stays in the executive branch is the enforcement capacity — the inspectors, field agents, and prosecutors who apply those specifications in the real world.

The specific transfers:

- From EPA: Office of Air and Radiation, Office of Chemical Safety and Pollution Prevention → CRO Directorates of Atmospheric Standards and Chemical Safety. The Office of Enforcement and Compliance Assurance stays in the Executive.  
- From FDA: Center for Drug Evaluation and Research, Center for Devices and Radiological Health → CRO Directorates of Pharmaceutical Specifications and Medical Engineering. The Office of Regulatory Affairs (inspections) stays in the Executive.  
- From DOT: NHTSA rulemaking division, FAA Aviation Safety Office → CRO Directorates of Transport Safety and Aerospace Standards.  
- From OMB: Office of Information and Regulatory Affairs → CRO Office of Quality Assurance.

This distinction — between the Architect and the Builder — is the structural spine of the entire framework.

**Congress, through the CRO, designs the specification.** It decides what PM2.5 level constitutes clean air, what clinical trial evidence justifies drug approval, what safety standard a drone must meet. These become statutes — the highest form of American law — not agency rules subject to reversal by the next administration.

**The Executive, through agencies and USDS, builds and enforces.** EPA inspectors enforce the clean air statute Congress wrote. FDA field offices inspect pharmaceutical manufacturers against specifications Congress enacted. The President retains full authority over the enforcement apparatus — hiring, firing, prioritization — while losing the authority to unilaterally rewrite what the law requires.

The constitutional objection addresses itself: critics of the administrative state have argued for decades that unelected bureaucrats shouldn't write law. Under this framework, they don't. Elected representatives do, with expert staff they control. *Loper Bright*'s requirement that courts interpret statutes precisely is not a crisis for this system — it's the design.

## **Two Swimlanes**

The CRO operates through a two-track legislative workflow — what the white paper calls Epics and Issues, or what software engineers would recognize as major releases and maintenance patches.

**Lane A: Major Legislation (Epics)**

For significant policy changes — new spending authorities, new rights, substantial tax changes — the process works as follows. A committee votes to commission a bill. The CRO drafts a first version based on committee intent. The committee holds hearings, markup, and revision. Then a classification vote:

- If the bill achieves **greater than 75% committee support**, it enters the Fast Lane: auto-ratified by the full House within 72 hours unless 20% of House members file a Petition of Dissent demanding floor debate.  
- If the bill achieves **less than 75%**, it enters the Standard Lane: traditional floor debate and voting.

The classification vote creates an incentive for genuine consensus-building rather than partisan minimum-winning. A bill that reaches 76% committee support gets to the floor in three days. A bill that passes on a 51-49 party-line vote faces the full traditional process.

**Lane B: Maintenance (Issues)**

For technical updates — updating a maximum contaminant level for arsenic, adjusting a drone clearance altitude, modifying a pharmaceutical stability testing parameter — the CRO submits a Pull Request to the committee's digital board. The process:

- **Silence is Consent:** If no member objects within 72 hours, the specification is deemed passed.  
- **Red Flag:** Any single member can flag a ticket, pulling it for the committee's next scheduled discussion.  
- **Batch Deployment:** Approved tickets are bundled weekly and enacted as statutory law unless 5% of the full House signs a Motion to Debate on a specific ticket.

This is not novel. The House has conducted business via unanimous consent since 1789 — a Speaker asks "is there objection?" and silence passes the motion. The digital board is unanimous consent at scale, with an authenticated record and a minority protection mechanism built in. *United States v. Ballin* (1892) established that the House has plenary authority to determine how it counts a quorum and records votes. The digital journal is a valid exercise of that authority.

The practical effect: Congress can process regulatory maintenance in real time rather than in multi-year cycles. An FDA drug approval standard that needs updating for a new delivery mechanism gets a CRO pull request, 72 hours of committee review, and a weekly batch enactment. The alternative is the current system, where updating a technical standard requires new legislation that may not move for a decade, leaving the country governed by standards written for a different era.

## **The Senate's New Role**

The Senate operates as a review body, not a co-equal origination chamber. The House is the engine; the Senate is the quality check.

Incoming House legislation is classified by the Parliamentarian:

**Class A (State Power):** Bills affecting state borders, budgets, or administrative capacity. The Senate has an **absolute veto** — an affirmative majority vote is required for passage.

**Class B (General Policy):** All other federal statutes. The Senate has a **Suspensive Veto** — but critically, it cannot simply vote "no." To stop a Class B bill, five senators must flag a specific defect and issue a **Remand Instruction** to the CRO: "Fix Section 4 to comply with the Fourth Amendment." "Revise the mercury standard — the CRO's methodology understates bioaccumulation in freshwater fish." The CRO patches the bill. The House accepts or rejects the patch. If House and Senate cannot agree, a Joint Conciliation Board of fifteen members from each chamber drafts a compromise and sends it to both chambers for an immediate up-or-down vote — no amendments.

The Remand Protocol eliminates the conference committee — the opaque backroom process where a handful of members rewrite legislation in secret, often at the behest of lobbyists who weren't elected to anything. Every objection, every patch, every resolution is on the public record. The CRO drafts corrections, not lobbyists in a conference room.

For nominations: Cabinet and Supreme Court nominees face full hearings and roll-call votes. Judges and agency heads receive committee affirmation with 72-hour floor window — silence equals confirmation. Military and civil service appointments are processed in batches; silence equals consent unless a member flags cause. The Senate stops spending weeks on each district court nominee and starts processing the backlog.

## **The Judiciary Gets Its Role Back**

The administrative state's most significant due process problem is one that rarely gets named directly: **in-house adjudication**. When the EPA fines a factory, the factory's appeal goes to an EPA Administrative Law Judge — an employee of the same agency that brought the charge. When the FTC pursues an antitrust case, its ALJ is an FTC employee. The SEC, CFPB, FCC, NLRB, and DOJ's immigration courts all operate the same way: the agency is simultaneously prosecutor and judge in its own cases.

This is not a procedural quirk. It is a structural violation of due process — an institution deciding the cases it brought — and it has survived largely because dismantling it would require somewhere for those cases to go.

The Governmental Efficacy Act provides that somewhere.

The position of Administrative Law Judge within the Executive Branch is abolished. All adjudicatory authority currently held by these agencies transfers to two new specialized Article III courts — judges with life tenure, compensation Congress cannot diminish, and no institutional interest in the outcome:

**The United States Immigration Court** assumes exclusive jurisdiction over removal proceedings, asylum claims, and visa disputes — cases previously decided by judges employed by the Department of Justice and subject to the Attorney General's authority to overrule their decisions, set their dockets, and terminate their positions. Under this framework, the AG has none of those powers. The court operates with a Chief Justice and approximately 300 Associate Justices organized into regional circuits.

**The United States Regulatory Court** assumes exclusive jurisdiction over civil enforcement actions by regulatory agencies — FTC antitrust, SEC securities fraud, CFPB consumer protection, EPA enforcement. An agency that believes a company violated the law must file suit in this court, as a plaintiff, and prove its case by preponderance of evidence before an independent judge. The agency cannot hold its own internal hearing and issue its own verdict. Any citizen or corporation charged with a regulatory violation has an absolute right to demand trial in this court rather than accept an informal agency settlement.

Crucially, this court applies **no deference** to agency interpretation. The *Loper Bright* problem that motivated this entire architecture — courts deferring to executive agencies on statutory meaning — is structurally resolved. The CRO writes precise statutes. The court interprets them as written, *de novo*, without asking what the EPA thinks Congress meant. The law means what Congress said, and Congress said it precisely.

The transition is managed through a four-year Magistrate Bridge: existing ALJs and immigration judges in good standing are provisionally deputized as federal magistrates while the President nominates and the Senate confirms permanent Article III judges. Current ALJs may be nominated but are not guaranteed seats.

The practical effect is that executive agencies are liberated from a role they were never constitutionally suited for. They become what the framework's architecture intends: expert enforcement bodies that build cases, present evidence, and accept whatever the independent court decides. They gain competence in prosecution. They lose the conflict of interest that has undermined their legitimacy for decades.

## **The Horseshoe Coalition**

The CRO proposal creates what the white paper calls a Horseshoe Coalition — groups that rarely agree finding common cause in the same structural fix, for different reasons.

**The conservative and libertarian right** — Kevin Kosar and Philip Wallach at AEI, the Federalist Society's Non-Delegation wing — have argued for years that the administrative state is constitutionally illegitimate because unelected bureaucrats are writing law that only elected representatives should write. The CRO gives them exactly what they want: Congress writes the law. Every regulation is a statute. The administrative state's constitutional foundations are restored, not dismantled.

**The state capacity progressives** — the Institute for Progress, Ezra Klein's "abundance" framework, Matt Yglesias and the Slow Boring readership — have argued that the progressive administrative state has become a machine for stopping things rather than building them. NEPA litigation blocks solar farms. APA challenges delay permitting for transit projects. The CRO's Fast Lane and Lane B maintenance system allows Congress to write precise, litigation-resistant statutes that can be built against immediately, without the years of regulatory interpretation that create lawsuit surface area.

**The institutionalists** — the Legislative Branch Capacity Working Group, the Select Committee on Modernization of Congress — simply want Congress to function. They have documented the staff hollowing-out of the past forty years: members relying on lobbyists to understand bills because they lack the expertise to evaluate them independently. The CRO is what rebuilds that capacity.

The grand bargain pitch to both sides is not deregulation and not big government. It is **competence.** A government that writes clear laws that agencies can execute, courts can interpret, and citizens can understand is better for everyone except the interests that profit from opacity.

## **We Have Done This Before**

The 1946 Legislative Reorganization Act is the direct precedent. Congress in the 1940s faced a comparable crisis: the New Deal and World War II had exploded the size of government far beyond what the existing legislative machinery could manage. Congress responded by slashing the number of committees, creating professional staff for the first time, and establishing the modern administrative procedure that governed the country for the next seventy years.

That Act built the administrative state. The Governmental Efficacy Act rebuilds the legislative one.

The UK did this in 1988 through the "Next Steps" reforms — separating policy ministries from executive delivery agencies, allowing the policy-writing function to become more expert and the delivery function to become more accountable. Goldwater-Nichols in 1986 did the same for the military: separated the people who design the force (Service Chiefs) from the people who fight with it (Combatant Commanders), ending the interservice rivalry that produced radios that couldn't communicate during the Grenada invasion.

Estonia runs its cabinet on a digital unanimous consent system — ministers review agenda items asynchronously before the weekly meeting; anything with unanimous digital agreement is already passed before the meeting convenes. The meeting addresses only flagged objections. Estonia is not a constitutional anomaly. It is proof that authenticated digital governance works.

## **The House: An Annuity, Not a Gamble**

Under the current system, a House member's career depends on a set of variables they cannot fully control: the lines a hostile state legislature may draw, a well-funded primary challenger from the party's activist base, a vote-splitting dynamic if a popular colleague runs in the same district. One bad cycle can end twenty years of work.

Under STV in a five-member district, the math changes structurally.

**The 17% threshold:** The Droop quota in a five-seat district is roughly 16.7%. A member who has spent years building constituent relationships — answering phones, attending town halls, sending casework staff to help navigate the VA — typically has name recognition that translates to 17% support across any district that contains their voters. They do not need to win a majority. They need to hold their base. The seat becomes an annuity: steady, predictable, not subject to the whims of a state legislature drawing lines at 2 a.m.

**Gerrymander insurance:** In a multi-member district, there are no lines to draw that eliminate a sitting member. If you represent the region, you win a seat in the region. A hostile state legislature can redraw the lines within the district. They cannot draw you out of it.

**The cannibalism end:** Currently, two popular incumbents from the same party in adjacent districts face a recurring death match — any consolidation of districts by the state legislature pits them against each other, with one career ending. Under ranked ballots, they run as partners: "Rank me first and her second." Their voters do the reverse. Both typically win. The zero-sum competition between colleagues in the same party dissolves.

**The fundraising ceasefire:** The current system requires members to spend 30 or more hours per week in call time — dialing donors from a room across the street from the Capitol because they cannot legally fundraise in the building. The Democracy Dollars system, combined with a ban on fundraising during legislative sessions, eliminates the permanent campaign. Members run for six weeks every two years and legislate the rest of the time. This is not a modest quality-of-life improvement. It is the recovery of the actual job.

**The power argument:** Under the current system, when a constituent calls to complain about an EPA rule, a member writes a letter to an administrator and hopes someone reads it. Under the CRO framework, the member's committee employs the architects who wrote the rule. They can direct a fix. The reform returns to Congress the actual levers of governance that four decades of delegation have transferred to the executive branch.

## **The Senate: The Murkowski Insurance Policy**

The Senate pitch centers on the threat every senator actually fears: not losing a general election, but being eliminated in a primary by the most activated 10% of the party base before the general electorate ever gets a vote.

**Top Five Primary:** Under the proposed open primary system, the five candidates with the most support advance to the general election, regardless of party. It is mathematically impossible for a sitting senator to fail to make the Top Five. The Eric Cantor scenario — a long-serving senior member eliminated in a primary shock by a well-funded ideological challenger — cannot happen. You are guaranteed a November ballot. You can vote for a bipartisan infrastructure bill without fearing that a primary challenger will use it to end your career.

**Center Squeeze protection:** Standard ranked-choice voting has a known flaw: it can eliminate the broadly acceptable candidate early if they lack first-choice intensity, even if they would beat everyone else head-to-head. Condorcet general elections are specifically designed to prevent this. If you are the candidate most voters would choose in a direct matchup — the "adult in the room" rather than the most ideologically activated option — you win. The system produces the senator who is broadly trusted rather than the one who has the most passionate minority.

**The mandate metric:** A senator who wins under the current system with 51% of the vote in a three-way race has a weak mandate and a large opposition that considers the result illegitimate. A senator who wins a Condorcet general election with a 4-0 record in head-to-head matchups — beating every opponent in a direct comparison — has demonstrated broad majority support in a verifiable way. They go back to Washington not as a survivor but as the demonstrated preference of a genuine majority of their state.

## **The Coalition That Exists Right Now**

The Callais decision and the redistricting wave it triggered have created an unusually motivated constituency for structural reform among the people who would need to vote for it.

Red-state Democratic members of Congress are watching their districts drawn out of existence in real time. Tennessee's 9th District — Memphis, majority-Black, Democratic-held for decades — was eliminated last week. Louisiana, Mississippi, Alabama, Florida, South Carolina are all redrawing. These members have nothing to lose from structural reform and everything to gain. Under STV in multi-member districts, a Democrat representing 25-30% of a deep-red Southern state still wins a seat. Under the current trajectory, they win nothing.

Blue-state Republican members in competitive districts understand the demographic math in their states. They have survived in districts that are trending against them by building local reputations and constituent relationships — exactly the kind of name recognition that the 17% threshold protects. They know that a less friendly redistricting cycle or a wave election could end careers that have survived through genuine constituent service. STV makes those careers structurally more secure.

The Indiana story from [Post 1](https://[your-substack-url]/p/the-map-they-drew) is the clarifying data point. Travis Holdman had served in the Indiana Senate since 2008 and was the third most powerful Republican in the chamber. He was eliminated in a primary for refusing to draw a map he considered inappropriate. He did not survive institutional good faith. Under STV and a Top Five primary structure, his constituent relationships would have protected him. The reform would not have endangered his career. The current system did.

## **What the Transition Actually Looks Like**

The standard fear about proportional representation is that it produces fragmented, ungovernable legislatures and hands power to fringe parties. The New Zealand precedent — the closest available evidence for what the American transition would look like — suggests the opposite.

New Zealand switched from first-past-the-post to proportional representation in 1996\. The first two electoral cycles were turbulent: parties fractured, coalition negotiations were unfamiliar, voters hadn't developed the strategic sophistication to use ranked ballots effectively. There was genuine buyer's remorse.

Then something happened. Voters learned that a vote for a smaller party wasn't wasted. Parties learned how to differentiate themselves meaningfully. Coalition governments became normalized. By the early 2000s, New Zealand was producing more representative outcomes than the old system had — with Labour and the National Party still dominating, still forming governments, still holding most seats.

The American transition would be rougher. The two-party brands are 150-year institutions with enormous organizational depth. The initial elections under STV would likely see Democrats and Republicans dominating by name recognition alone — which is precisely why the reform is sellable to incumbents. Their names are the ones voters know. The 17% threshold combined with name recognition is, for most sitting members, a structural guarantee that the transition does not end their careers. What it ends is the career of the challenger who wins with 22% in a crowded primary by activating the most extreme 10% of the base.

## **What This Series Was Arguing**

Six posts ago, the map was being drawn while the court was still speaking. Florida passed a new congressional map within an hour of the *Callais* decision. Tennessee eliminated a majority-Black district. Five Indiana senators who showed institutional good faith were purged by a primary system doing exactly what it was designed to do.

Those are not isolated events. They are the logical outputs of a system operating correctly according to its own rules — rules that reward intensity over representation, geographic manipulation over voter preference, and minority-faction capture over democratic accountability. The simulation showed that the faction currently running the most powerful government in human history would hold 22 House seats under fair democratic competition. They know this. The redistricting wave and the assault on democratic institutions are not aberrations. They are strategy.

The design proposed across these six posts is not utopian. It is not even particularly radical by the standards of functioning democracies — Ireland, Germany, New Zealand, and dozens of others use proportional representation and produce governments that roughly reflect what their electorates believe. What is radical is the claim that the United States, with its specific constitutional structure and 150-year two-party institutional depth, can move toward such a system.

The claim is defensible. The path is narrow. The window, given the Callais cascade and the redistricting entrenchment currently underway, is not permanently open.

The architecture exists. The coalition exists. The empirical case exists. What it needs is the political moment — and the political moment is now.
